# Slide 11 — Teacher decisions and Kev distillation

The teacher can spend time exploring several futures before it chooses a move. Our student needs to make that choice quickly during play. We connect those two pieces by saving the teacher’s chosen action beside the exact decision that produced it, then teaching Kev to give that action a higher probability.

Start on the left. The letter s is the full observed game state: the complete maze, Pac-Man, all four ghosts, their movement and timing fields, and the recent history. The letter u is the decision question. Capital A is the original ordered list of legal action options. We preserve the same state, question and options for the student. The teacher’s simulated future outcomes stay in the evidence files. They do not become extra clues in Kev’s input.

The teacher box gives us a sub T, written a_T. T identifies the teacher, and a is one action from those legal options. In the public example from the last slide, the teacher chose Left. That recorded teacher choice is real. Seed 91009 belongs to qualification and stays excluded from our training data. We use it here to explain how a label works.

We also have to decide which teacher labels deserve to enter the dataset. The frozen teacher cleared its finite 20-game qualification suite without a life loss in this run. The aggregate qualification rule permits up to four losses, so that result is better than its required threshold. Positive training continuations face a stricter individual rule: each one must finish the native maze with zero life loss, pass the original loop and stall gates, and reproduce under an independent cold replay with matching source hashes. These checks give evidence for the admitted cases. They do not establish that the teacher is optimal on every board.

Now move to Kev on the right. Phi, the Greek letter φ, stands for the weights we allow this stage to change: the LoRA matrices and the pointer head. We initialize them from the completed native-v3 checkpoint. W with a zero below it, W₀, means the original backbone matrices. Those remain fixed. A new v4 stage starts a fresh optimizer and schedule rather than inheriting v3’s optimizer state. Resuming an interrupted v4 run is different: recovery restores that run’s optimizer and completed step.

Kev produces p sub phi. That is its probability distribution over the supplied actions, after the pointer head scores them and softmax normalizes the scores. To make the calculation easy to follow, the slide gives Left a probability of 0.20, Up 0.35 and Right 0.45. These are illustrative numbers. They are not measured predictions from either checkpoint.

The equation tells us how one Pac-Man choice contributes to the training loss. Script L, ℒ, is the loss, and the phi in parentheses reminds us that it depends on the trainable weights. We look up the probability that Kev assigns to the teacher action, a sub T. The vertical bar means “given”: given this state s, question u and option list A. We take its natural logarithm and put a minus sign in front. A probability of 0.20 gives about 1.61. If Kev instead assigned 0.80 to that same target, the loss would be about 0.22. The controls let us change that probability while keeping the complete distribution normalized.

That calculation uses one action label. We do not copy the teacher’s search ranking into a soft probability target, and this recipe does not use a teacher KL loss. During training, Kev learns from saved labels. During live play, Kev chooses the action itself. The offline teacher does not override its moves. The next slide will show how the loss reaches the pointer head and LoRA matrices, so we can leave the gradient mechanics there.

Finally, look at the data counts. We selected 3,660 targeted roots and 1,220 nearby informative decisions. We retained 912 canonical native-v2 expert replay records. Those make 5,792 Pac-Man task records. Kev adds 304 generic decision replay requests, giving 6,096 requests and 762 optimizer updates for one epoch at batch four with accumulation two. All 11 recorded-v3 disagreement roots are already inside those counts. Other hard cases come from native simulation and counterfactual tests. We should not describe all of them as mistakes that we observed v3 make.

The interactive page shows the category breakdown, including the limited 16 training and four development examples of actual native power expiry. These counts describe the coverage we have, not a promise that every behavior is solved. A low choice loss tells us that Kev matches labels better on those examples. Ghost evasion, loops and pellet efficiency still need measurement in complete games.
