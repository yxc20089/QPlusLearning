We now know what Kev sees at a decision boundary. To teach it a good direction, we need a teacher that can compare what happens next. Our teacher does this by running the actual game forward in several temporary forecasts.

Follow the diagram from the snapshot on the left. We call the complete native game state g-sub-t. The subscript t means the current decision. The teacher saves the actors, the maze and the engine’s internal clocks. It also carries forward the recent movement history. Without that history, a short forecast can keep promising food while the real player repeatedly reverses in the same corridor.

Each branch starts with one legal direction. In this fresh seed-seven example, Pac-Man has moved Left once from the opening position. At row twenty-six, column twelve, the next legal directions are Up, Left and Right. We restore the same snapshot before trying each one.

After that first move, a routing policy chooses the later moves. It uses cached shortest distances through the maze to find food and account for dangerous ghosts. We try four versions of that policy, controlled by the buffer b: one, two, four or six maze steps. The buffer changes the policy’s danger and food-route preferences. It is not a guaranteed safe radius. At each later decision, the policy simulates the legal one-action transitions and prefers those that survive immediately. It then picks a continuation using its routing rule.

For each buffer, we also try two future random sequences. The normal ghost personalities still follow the engine’s targeting rules. Frightened turns can depend on randomness, so two sampled futures can expose different outcomes. These are fresh planning samples. The teacher does not read the real episode’s future random sequence.

The orange box is the native game engine. It advances the pixels and clocks, including ghost release, chase and scatter, frightened time and eating pauses. It checks collisions using the same mechanics as interactive play. We normally forecast two hundred and forty active game frames. We extend that to four hundred and eighty near a dangerous ghost, or when thirty or fewer pellets remain. A movement finishes at its native boundary, so a forecast can go a little beyond that frame limit.

How do we compare the results? Rho, the Greek letter in the ranking box, represents an ordered vector of criteria. We compare its components from first to last. Immediate death comes first. Closing an existing dry cycle and predicting an imminent cycle come next. Then we consider deaths across the two futures and survival duration, followed by time to the next pellet and the other progress terms. A later food reward cannot cancel an earlier immediate death. This ordered comparison is called lexicographic ranking.

The numbers on the right make it concrete. For the selected Up, Left and Right forecasts, the first five components all tie at zero. We therefore compare the next-pellet times. Up takes seven frames, Left takes nine and Right takes thirty-one. Up wins. The interactive page lets us change the routing buffer or future sample and inspect the whole rank.

The equation expresses that choice. A-sub-t is the set of legal directions at this decision. The small a is one direction from that set, and b is one of our four routing buffers. Rho of g-sub-t, a and b is the rank produced by their forecasts. Argmin selects the pair with the smallest rank. The small lex tells us to compare its components in order. On the left, a-sub-t with a superscript T is the teacher’s selected direction. T stands for teacher. b-sub-t with a star is its selected buffer. The code first chooses the best buffer for each direction, then the best direction. That gives the same minimum over this finite set of candidates.

Planning has not advanced the real game. We restore the live root, execute only Up, then read the new state and plan again. This repeated forecast-and-replan process is model predictive control, or MPC. Its forecast is approximate because we use a finite policy portfolio and horizon.
